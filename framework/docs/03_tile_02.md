# Tile 2 Spec: Residential Urban Landscape

## General Tile Identity
- Tile Name: Tile 2
- Tile Type: Tertiary (Middle zone, bridge between coastal hillside and fully urbanized residential area)
- Primary Visuals: Continuing the coastal hill descent from Tile 1, seamlessly integrating into the residential neighborhood of Tile 3. Features include layered streets, residential homes, local commerce, greenery, small lakes, and water features. New elements include small urban bridges and tunnels linking different street levels.

## Key Features

### 1. Hill Continuation
- The hill from Tile 1 continues but begins a gentle descent towards the horizon line. The slope is not steep, but still noticeable, with layered streets following the hill’s contours.
- The hilltop should not be visible, but it should blend with the background, showing how the urban fabric grew organically on the land. The horizontal horizon line remains clear and even on the right side, with the left side maintaining the higher hill structure.

### 2. Residential Features
- Urban homes should feature a mix of architectural styles: some older homes, mid-century buildings, and a few modern homes. The streets should be lined with residential homes that descend along the slope, showing a gradual transition toward Tile 3.
- Local commerce should be visible at street level, including small shops, markets, cafes, and possibly corner stores.
- Streets should descend toward the horizon line, and these streets will be lined with urban homes of various sizes. The street grid should remain organic, with narrow avenues interspersed with larger roads that transition smoothly into Tile 3.

### 3. Small Urban Bridges and Tunnels
- Small urban bridges should be scattered throughout the residential area, connecting different street levels. These urban-grade bridges should span small canals, irrigation channels, or shallow valleys, creating connections between elevated streets and lower streets.
- Bridges should be modest in size, with arched concrete supports or steel structures that suit the residential nature of the tile. 
- Small tunnels could also connect different street layers, especially in areas where terrain creates significant vertical drop-offs between residential sections. These urban tunnels should feel integrated into the streetscape and should blend seamlessly with the surroundings, like underpasses or subterranean paths for pedestrians.

### 4. Greenery and Nature
- Interspersed throughout the residential streets are small parks, community gardens, and street trees that blend into the residential fabric. These elements of greenery should be subtly integrated into the urban fabric, showcasing the balance between nature and urban life.
- Small lakes, ponds, and water management systems like irrigation channels or concrete vertical waterlines should be scattered through the tile, blending with both the residential and green areas.
  - Allowed water features are SMALL and local: ponds, small lakes, irrigation channels, drainage runs.
  - Forbidden: navigable channel/harbor/coastline continuation into Tile 2; no ships/tankers.

### 5. Urban Details
- Street elements such as sidewalks, curbside greenery, streetlights, and signposts should populate the scene, showing the urban fabric at the human level.
- Older-style public transport buses should be visible, serving local routes. The buses should feel retro, fitting the vintage charm of the neighborhood.
- Supermarket or large market: A medium to large supermarket should be placed in a street-level location, possibly with a small parking lot. It should stand out as a commercial building, blending into the residential area but large enough to be distinct from the houses.

### Subtle Architectural Plurality
- Introduce small traces of East Asian residential architecture as part of the normal mixed neighborhood fabric.
- Allowed: a few Chinese- or Japanese-influenced houses, tiled roof silhouettes, compact walled homes, courtyard-like residential compounds, narrow low-rise mixed-use shop houses.
- These elements must remain subtle, scattered, and integrated into the city fabric.
- They should read as neighborhood variation, not as a declared themed enclave.
- Forbidden: oversized gates, pagodas, shrine/temple hero objects, lantern-heavy tourism imagery, or any overt "Chinatown / Little Tokyo" postcard read.

### 6. Horizon and Sky
- The horizon line should remain at the top-right of the scene, with the hill slope disappearing into the background. The skyline should be unobstructed, with the residential fabric gently transitioning from hilly terrain into more urban infrastructure.
- There should be no visible sun disk, but light should come from the right, casting subtle shadows toward the left. This helps emphasize the late-afternoon lighting that blends with the rest of the tiles.

### 7. Seam and Tile Continuity
- Seam continuity should be maintained with Tile 1 to the left, ensuring there is a smooth connection from the hillside into Tile 2. 
- The transition into Tile 3 on the right should continue the residential elements while introducing slightly denser urban structures as the slope flattens.
- Ensure the small urban bridges and tunnels do not disrupt the seam lock or break continuity, instead acting as subtle transitional elements between different street levels.

## Seam & Stitching Details

### Seam Strip Lock (Pixel-based for 1024×1536)
- Leftmost 120px and rightmost 120px are seam-blending strips: use low-uniqueness textures like trees, small houses, street elements, and community spaces to avoid breaking the continuity.
- LEFT seam must remain especially quiet against Tile 1:
  - no road centerline or strong roadway ribbon entering/exiting the frame on the left edge
  - no bridge deck, tunnel mouth, or hard linear infrastructure touching the left seam
  - prefer vegetation, small retaining walls, small houses, slope texture, and minor street fragments only
- Small bridges and tunnels are allowed inside the tile, but they must stay away from the leftmost seam strip and must not become seam-driving elements.
